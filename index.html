About

Krys Reyes resides in the Chicago metropolitan area. Introduced to videogames from a young age–and soon after, tabletop games–he greatly enjoys writing about and analyzing existing games. While passionate about the arts and humanities, Krys also has a background in mathematics and computer science, which complements his interest in exploring the technical details of games design, as well his reverence towards progress and innovation.

Essays

My writing explores the history and development of individual works, genres, and mediums. By presenting games from a historical perspective, I credit the important contributions of individual creators on game culture and design philosophy, while also emphasizing the collective and iterative nature of art creation. The central theme across my work is that no art exists in isolation. By analyzing individual case studies and chronicling the influences, changes, and trends in games, my writing helps identify their core elements to draw important lessons in game design, while stressing the potential for future innovation.
Adapting Nonsense Literature Into Point-and-Click Adventure Games
Fran Bow has long been one of my favorite video games, and the point-and-click adventure being a genre I greatly enjoy. When I first learned of the history of Fran Bow, as well as the history of Mystery House, the predecessor to the point-and-click adventure genre, I could not help but think that the two origins were remarkably similar, even astoundingly so. With these similarities in mind, I wondered what made Fran Bow stand out compared to other games of its kind, as well as why it was so well received at a time when the genre had largely diminished in popularity. In my investigations, I learned that while Fran Bow partakes in many of the genre's longstanding conventions, it also tells a story that is uniquely suited to the structure of a point-and-click adventure, breathing new life into the genre.
Longing in Fake Video Games and Conceptualization as a Ludic Activity
Fake games can briefly be described as works of art created as secondary material to games that don’t actually exist. They vary in execution, but examples can include a game manual or a song album stylized as a soundtrack to a nonexistent game. Fake games are fascinating to me. I am fond of them simply for their variety of aesthetically pleasing and well-crafted works of art. However, I also find their existence to be somewhat paradoxical. Fake video games discard, what I believe to be, one of the fundamental aspects of what constitutes a video game, that of their interactability and responsiveness to player input. Why then are there “players” who choose to consume fake video games in lieu of playable video games? Why do creators invest, often immense amounts of, time and effort into creating a fake game game instead of a playable game? In my investigations, I found that by completely omitting a playable experience, the “player” is obliged to reconstruct their own highly individualistic vision of what the game could look like. As it turns out, this creative process is the main appeal of fake video games, and can be taken as a playful activity, not unlike roleplay or worldbuilding.
Early Creative Influences of D&D and Restrictions on Player Freedom
As an avid player of Dungeons & Dragons (D&D), I am well aware of the immense impact that this game has had on not just other games, but also fantasy and culture at large. Noting how much of a massive inspiration D&D has been to other works, I, in turn, wondered what could inspire such a cultural touchstone. Thus, I set out to investigate the original inspirations of D&D, asking if these influences can still be felt in modern iterations of the game. In doing so, I observed an interesting pattern stemming from D&D’s somewhat incongruous nature. As a collaborative sandbox roleplaying game, the game is bound by its systems and rules, but simultaneously values player freedom and expression. Many of the rules inspired by preexisting tabletop games, specifically the miniature wargame genre, remain largely recognizable and have even become fundamental to the TTRPG genre. Meanwhile, many of the rules inspired by fantasy literature, especially those relating to character tropes and magic, have been heavily revised, as they were adapted in a far too restrictive manner.
